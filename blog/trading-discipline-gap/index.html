The Discipline Gap: Why Capable Traders Break Rules They Fully Understand

Trading losses are rarely caused by a lack of knowledge. They come from what happens to judgement under pressure. The fix is rehearsal, not more reading.

By Sam Onigbanjo, founder of Capital Markets Academy and creator of the EIT™ framework · 18 November 2026 · 6 min read

Over the years I have sat in the same room as intelligent, well-paid professionals who were losing money in the markets to behavioural patterns they did not understand. They were not careless or uninformed. Many had already invested in trading education elsewhere before joining my community. When I looked closely at where the money actually went, the pattern was consistent. They knew what to do, and in the moment that mattered, they did something else.

Behavioural scientists call this the intention-action gap: the distance between what we plan to do and what we actually do. In trading, most of the damage happens inside that gap.

Why knowing is not enough

Most traders know that moving a stop loss to avoid a loss is not always a good idea. A few days later, faced with a Wednesday afternoon trading opportunity, the same trader does exactly that.

This is not hypocrisy. It is how humans are wired. Kahneman and Tversky showed that losses feel heavier than equivalent gains, a bias known as loss aversion. In markets it shows up as the disposition effect: we sell winners too early and hold losers too long, hoping not to have to admit the loss.

The economist George Loewenstein named another trap, the hot-cold empathy gap. When we are calm, we consistently underestimate how differently we will behave when we are anxious or afraid. The trader who writes a careful plan on Sunday evening is, in a real sense, not the same decision-maker who executes it midweek.

So the problem is not information. It is behaviour under stress. Behaviour is not changed by being told things. It is changed by practice.

Three moments that reveal your trading behaviour

When you play, watch for three moments. Together they form a simple diagnostic.

  1. Your first instinct. Is it a plan or a reaction? This shows whether you are deciding or responding.
  2. The moment you break your own rule. What triggered it? A loss, a fast move, boredom? This is your personal pressure point.
  3. What you do straight after a loss. Do you reset, or do you try to win it back? Of the three, this tells you the most about your future results.

Once you have identified your trigger, turn it into an implementation intention, a technique studied extensively by psychologist Peter Gollwitzer. Write a specific "if X, then Y" rule, for example: "If I take a loss, then I step away from the screen for 15 minutes." Pre-committed responses hold up under pressure far better than general resolutions.

It is far cheaper to meet that version of yourself in a simulation than in your brokerage account.
